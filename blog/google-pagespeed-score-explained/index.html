Google PageSpeed score explained in plain English

You paste your website into Google's PageSpeed Insights, wait a few seconds, and get a big coloured number. Maybe it's a red 34. Maybe an orange 61. Then comes a long list of warnings with names like "Largest Contentful Paint" and "Reduce unused JavaScript".

What does it all actually mean, and how worried should you be? Here's the plain-English version.

Where to test

Go to PageSpeed Insights, paste in your website address and press Analyze. You'll get two tabs: Mobile and Desktop. Start with Mobile. Most small business visitors are on a phone, and Google mainly looks at the mobile version of your site when deciding how to rank it.

The two halves of the report

This is the part most people miss. The page shows two different kinds of results.

1. "Discover what your real users are experiencing": this is field data. It comes from real visits to your site by people using Chrome, collected over the last 28 days. If your site doesn't get much traffic, this section may say there isn't enough data. That's normal for a small business site.

2. "Diagnose performance issues": this is lab data. Google loads your page once, right now, on a simulated mid-range phone with a slowish mobile connection. The big score out of 100 comes from this test.

So the big number is a single test in artificial conditions. It's a very useful guide, but it isn't the same as what your customers actually experience.

What the score colours mean

  • 0–49 (red): slow. Visitors are likely giving up before the page loads.
  • 50–89 (orange): okay, with room to improve.
  • 90–100 (green): fast.

Don't be surprised if you run the test twice and get two different scores. A swing of 5 or 10 points between runs is normal, because the lab test is sensitive to tiny differences in network and server timing. Run it three times and look at the middle result.

Why mobile is lower than desktop

Mobile scores are nearly always lower, sometimes by 30 points or more. That doesn't mean your site is broken on phones. The mobile test deliberately slows down the processor and connection to mimic an average phone on 4G, so every heavy image and script hurts more. Treat the mobile score as your real target.

The Core Web Vitals, decoded

Google's Core Web Vitals are the three measurements it cares most about. They appear in the field data section, and as "Passed" or "Failed" at the top.

Largest Contentful Paint (LCP): how fast does the main thing appear? This is the time until the biggest item on screen, usually your hero image or main heading, has loaded. Good is 2.5 seconds or less. A huge, uncompressed header photo is the most common reason this fails.

Interaction to Next Paint (INP): does it respond when I tap? This measures the delay between someone tapping a button or menu and the page visibly reacting. Good is 200 milliseconds or less. Heavy scripts, chat widgets and bloated page builders are the usual suspects.

Cumulative Layout Shift (CLS): does the page jump around? You've seen this: you go to tap a link and the page jumps because an image or banner loaded late, so you tap the wrong thing. Good is 0.1 or less. Images without set sizes, late-loading cookie banners and web fonts swapping in are common causes.

The other numbers in the lab test

The lab section also shows First Contentful Paint (when anything first appears), Speed Index (how quickly the page visibly fills in) and Total Blocking Time (how long the page is too busy to respond). The big score is a weighted mix of these plus LCP and CLS. Total Blocking Time, LCP and CLS carry the most weight, so improving those moves the score fastest.

How to read the long list of warnings

Under the scores you'll see Opportunities and Diagnostics. Don't try to fix everything. Look at the first few opportunities, because they're sorted by how much time they could save. On a typical WordPress site you'll see the same few again and again:

  1. Properly size images / Serve images in next-gen formats: your photos are too big. Resize them and convert them to WebP.
  2. Eliminate render-blocking resources: scripts and fonts are holding up the page. A good caching plugin can defer them.
  3. Reduce unused JavaScript / CSS: your theme or plugins load code on pages that don't need it.
  4. Reduce initial server response time: your hosting is slow to respond, or there's no caching.

What's a realistic target? For a typical small business WordPress site, aim for 70+ on mobile and passing Core Web Vitals. That's a site that feels quick to real visitors. Going from 75 to 95 is often a lot of work for very little real-world difference.

Should you chase 100?

Usually not. A perfect score is nice, but real business sites have maps, booking widgets, reviews and analytics, and each one costs a few points. Removing useful features just to hit a number can lose you more customers than it wins. Be wary of anyone who promises a perfect score whatever your site has on it.

What matters is this: does the page appear quickly, respond when tapped, and stay still while it loads? If the answer is yes, you're in good shape.
